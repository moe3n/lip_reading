# Chapter 3. Background

This chapter provides the technical background needed to follow the rest of the dissertation: how an automated lip-reading system is structured and where the phoneme-to-text stage sits within it, the language models used for that stage and the methods used to adapt them efficiently, and the homophone problem that motivated part of the original research framing.

## 3.1 Lip-reading pipelines and the phoneme-to-text stage

Automated lip-reading recovers text from silent or degraded video of a speaker's face. Early systems mapped video directly to characters or words with a single end-to-end network, such as the Watch, Listen, Attend and Spell model that accompanied the LRS dataset (Chung et al., 2017) and the deep audio-visual models that followed (Afouras et al., 2018). These systems learn the whole task at once, which couples the visual problem of reading the face to the linguistic problem of producing correct text.

A second design separates these problems into a pipeline. A visual front-end first predicts a sequence of speech units from the video, commonly phonemes, the smallest sound segments of a language, represented here in the ARPAbet notation used for English, or visemes, the visually distinguishable mouth shapes. A decoder then converts the unit sequence into words. Separating the stages lets each be studied and improved on its own, and it isolates a specific weakness: several studies report that the conversion from recognised units to text limits sentence-level accuracy even when the front-end performs well (El-Ghomari et al., 2024). This dissertation works entirely on the second stage, taking phoneme sequences as given and studying how well a language model converts them to text, without touching the visual front-end.

The phoneme-to-text stage is close to a translation task. A phoneme sequence for an English sentence encodes it almost completely, because the sounds determine the words up to a limited set of ambiguities. Two remain: spelling, since English does not map sounds to letters consistently, and homophony, since different words can share a pronunciation. Apart from these, the mapping is close to deterministic, which is why a model with strong knowledge of English word sequences is well suited to the task.

A practical consequence is that the quality of the phoneme input matters. Ideally the decoder receives the exact phoneme sequence; in a deployed system the phonemes come from the visual front-end, which makes mistakes, labelling a sound as the wrong phoneme, missing a sound entirely, or inserting a sound that was not spoken. A decoder only ever trained and tested on perfect phonemes is therefore evaluated under conditions it will not meet in practice. This gap between clean training input and noisy deployment input motivates the robustness experiments in this project.

## 3.2 Large language models and sequence transduction

A language model assigns probabilities to sequences of words or tokens, and a large language model is one with many parameters trained on a large text corpus. Two architectural families are relevant. Encoder-decoder models such as T5 read an input with an encoder and generate an output with a decoder, designed with sequence-to-sequence tasks such as translation and summarisation in mind (Raffel et al., 2020). Decoder-only models such as the Llama family use a single stack that predicts each token from the tokens before it, and have become the dominant design for general-purpose language modelling (Grattafiori et al., 2024). The prior phoneme-to-text work on LRS2 used encoder-decoder models (El-Ghomari et al., 2024); this project uses a decoder-only model, framing phoneme-to-text conversion as the completion of a prompt containing the phonemes.

Adapting a large pretrained model by retraining all of its parameters, full fine-tuning, is expensive in memory and computation and becomes impractical as models grow. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) freezes the pretrained weights and inserts small trainable matrices whose product approximates the change in a weight matrix; because these have low rank, they add very few trainable parameters yet recover most of the benefit of full fine-tuning (Hu et al., 2021). QLoRA extends this by first quantising the frozen model to four-bit precision, reducing its memory footprint further and allowing a multi-billion-parameter model to be fine-tuned on a single consumer graphics card while retaining the accuracy of higher-precision training (Dettmers et al., 2023). This project uses QLoRA to fine-tune Llama-3.2-3B, keeping the whole process within a single available GPU.

At generation time a language model produces text one token at a time, and the decoding strategy governs how each token is chosen. Greedy decoding takes the single most probable token at every step; beam search keeps several candidate sequences and returns the highest-scoring completed one, which can recover from an early choice that greedy decoding would commit to. Because decoding is separate from the trained model and can change the output substantially, it is treated as an experimental variable in this work.

A further relevant technique is data augmentation for robustness. In speech recognition, training on deliberately perturbed inputs is a long-standing way to make a model tolerant of the distortions it will meet at test time; SpecAugment, which masks and warps the input features during training, is a widely used modern example (Park et al., 2019). The same principle applies here: corrupting a fraction of the training phonemes as a visual front-end would teaches the decoder to recover the intended sentence from imperfect input. This project applies that idea and measures its effect.

## 3.3 The homophone hypothesis

Homophones are words that share a pronunciation but differ in spelling and meaning, such as "there", "their", and "they're", or "to", "too", and "two". Because a phoneme sequence captures pronunciation and not spelling, a homophone is invisible at the phoneme level: the correct and incorrect words produce the same phonemes, so only the surrounding context can separate them. Homophones are therefore a natural candidate for the hardest residual errors in phoneme-to-text conversion, and a recognised difficulty in lip-reading more broadly, where the related notion of homophenes, different sounds that look the same on the lips, has motivated dedicated model components (Kim et al., 2022).

The early framing of this problem treated homophones as a likely priority. Whether homophones actually dominate the errors of a fine-tuned decoder is an empirical question, however, and it can be answered by classifying the model's mistakes. A prior study in the group already provided evidence on this point: in its error analysis of a Flan-T5 decoder on LRS2, homophone confusions accounted for only about 1% of the mistakes, well below contextual and phonetic errors (Hossain et al., under review). Chapter 10 tests the same question for the decoder-only model used here, across both a clean-trained and a noise-augmented decoder, and reaches the same conclusion.
