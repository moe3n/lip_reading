# Chapter 4. Literature Review

This chapter reviews the research relevant to phoneme-to-text conversion in lip-reading and positions the present work within it. It compares the architectures, adaptation methods, and evaluation practices of competing approaches, weighs their strengths against their limitations, and links each limitation to a decision made here. It also sets out the legal, social, ethical, and professional issues that arise in working with a corpus of recorded faces and with technology that can read speech from video.

## 4.1 Phoneme-based lip-reading and the two-stage pipeline

Early deep-learning lip-reading systems mapped video directly to text with a single network. The Watch, Listen, Attend and Spell model that accompanied the LRS dataset transcribed mouth motion to characters end to end and set the benchmark still used today (Chung et al., 2017), and the deep audio-visual models that followed extended this to larger networks and the joint use of audio and video (Afouras et al., 2018). Optimising the whole task at once is their strength, but it entangles the visual problem of reading the face with the linguistic problem of producing correct text in one set of weights, so it is hard to tell which of the two limits performance.

A two-stage design separates these problems: a visual front-end predicts a sequence of speech units, and a second module converts the units to text. Recent systems place a language model in that second stage. VALLR predicts phonemes from video and reconstructs sentences with a language model, reporting that the phoneme intermediate representation improves data efficiency (Thomas et al., 2025), while VSP-LLM uses discretised visual speech units as an interface to a large language model and translates lip movements from far less labelled data than earlier systems (Yeo et al., 2024). For the present work the value of this design is that it isolates the phoneme-to-text stage as an object of study. Several of these studies also note that the second stage limits overall accuracy, the observation the group's own work makes directly (El-Ghomari et al., 2024).

The main limitation of this literature, for present purposes, is that the phoneme-to-text stage is almost always evaluated inside a full system rather than on its own. This couples its measured accuracy to the particular visual front-end used and leaves the decoder's own behaviour, its error profile and its response to imperfect input, largely unexamined. This project studies the stage in isolation, with the phonemes given, so its behaviour can be observed without the confound of a specific front-end.

A related strand concerns homophenes, different sounds that produce the same visible mouth shape and are therefore indistinguishable to a visual-only system; Kim et al. (2022) address these with a multi-head visual-audio memory that retrieves candidate pronunciations at inference time. Homophenes are a genuine difficulty for the visual front-end, but they differ from homophones at the phoneme-to-text stage, where the phonemes are already given and the remaining ambiguity is between words that share a pronunciation. The distinction matters here because the error analysis in Chapter 10 concerns homophones at the text stage rather than homophenes at the visual stage.

## 4.2 Language models for phoneme-to-text and phonetic conversion

Two architectural families turn phoneme sequences into text. Encoder-decoder models read the input with an encoder and generate the output with a separate decoder, a design introduced for sequence-to-sequence problems and embodied in the T5 family (Raffel et al., 2020). Decoder-only models use a single stack that predicts each token from the preceding ones, the design of the Llama family and most recent general-purpose language models (Grattafiori et al., 2024).

On this task the existing evidence favours encoder-decoder models, but only because decoder-only models have scarcely been tried. El-Ghomari et al. (2024) compared T5 with GPT-2 for phoneme-to-text translation on LRS2 and found T5 clearly better, and the follow-up study fine-tuned Flan-T5 Large with LoRA to reach 85.11% exact match with a 3.23% word error rate on the LRS2 test set (Hossain et al., under review). Both are encoder-decoder models. GPT-2, the one decoder-only model tried, was early and comparatively weak, so its poor result does not settle whether a modern decoder-only model would succeed. The present work addresses this gap by fine-tuning Llama-3.2-3B, a current decoder-only model, and framing the task as prompt completion.

Beyond lip-reading, language models have been applied to closely related phonetic conversion problems, which supports their use at this stage. Fetrat Qharabagh et al. (2024) showed that a prompted language model converts graphemes to phonemes more accurately than traditional tools for an under-resourced language, without task-specific training, and work replacing weighted finite-state transducer decoders in speech recognition with a language-model-based phoneme-to-grapheme module has reported lower error rates than the transducer. These results indicate that language models capture the phonological and lexical regularities phoneme-to-text conversion needs, and motivate applying a strong decoder-only model to the visual-speech setting.

## 4.3 Parameter-efficient fine-tuning

Adapting a large pretrained model by retraining all of its weights is expensive and grows harder as models grow. Low-Rank Adaptation freezes the pretrained weights and inserts small trainable matrices whose low-rank product approximates the weight change, cutting trainable parameters by orders of magnitude while recovering most of the benefit of full fine-tuning (Hu et al., 2021). QLoRA reduces memory further by quantising the frozen model to four-bit precision first, which lets a multi-billion-parameter model be fine-tuned on a single consumer graphics card while retaining the accuracy of higher-precision training (Dettmers et al., 2023).

The prior lip-reading study used LoRA on Flan-T5 Large, updating under 1% of the parameters (Hossain et al., under review), and VSP-LLM likewise used LoRA (Yeo et al., 2024); neither used four-bit quantisation. Quantisation is not free, as four-bit weights lose precision, and whether that loss harms a task as near-deterministic as phoneme-to-text conversion is a fair question. This work adopts QLoRA to keep a 3-billion-parameter decoder-only model within the single available GPU, and reports whether the resulting accuracy is competitive, the practical test of whether the precision loss matters here.

## 4.4 Robustness and data augmentation

A model trained only on clean input is not guaranteed to tolerate the distorted input it meets in deployment. In speech recognition, training on deliberately perturbed inputs is a standard remedy: SpecAugment masks and warps the input features during training and improves recognition of unseen, distorted speech without changing the model (Park et al., 2019). The principle is general, in that showing a model the corruption it will face at test time teaches it to recover.

For the phoneme-to-text stage this principle has not, to the knowledge of this review, been applied: the existing studies train and test their decoders on clean phoneme transcriptions (El-Ghomari et al., 2024; Hossain et al., under review). In a full system the phonemes come from an imperfect front-end that substitutes, drops, and inserts them, so a decoder evaluated only on clean input is tested under conditions it will not meet. This project applies the augmentation principle here by corrupting a fraction of the training phonemes with the same three operations a front-end produces, and measures the effect on robustness against a clean-trained decoder. The term deduplication also appears in this literature in a different sense: VSP-LLM deduplicates repeated visual speech units to shorten its input (Yeo et al., 2024), whereas here it removes evaluation sentences that also appear in training, a matter of honest measurement rather than efficiency.

## 4.5 Evaluation and error analysis

Phoneme-to-text systems are commonly reported with word error rate, character error rate, and sentence-level exact match, sometimes with a BLEU score for fluency (El-Ghomari et al., 2024; Hossain et al., under review). These metrics measure surface agreement between prediction and reference. They are necessary but not sufficient, treating every word substitution as equally bad and saying nothing about why an error occurred; two systems with the same word error rate can fail in entirely different ways, one making systematic and correctable confusions and the other producing scattered noise.

Semantic evaluation addresses part of this. BERTScore compares prediction and reference using contextual token embeddings rather than exact matches, and correlates better with human judgement than surface metrics (Zhang et al., 2020). Sentence-BERT encodes each whole sentence to a single vector and compares the vectors, measuring whether two sentences mean the same thing rather than whether they share words (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019). The two are not interchangeable, which matters here: a token-level score can stay high when meaning has changed, because it rewards word overlap, whereas a sentence-level score is more sensitive to it. This project uses both and reports where they disagree.

For error analysis proper, machine translation offers structured taxonomies. The Multidimensional Quality Metrics framework classifies errors by type and severity, separating accuracy from fluency and grading each error (Lommel et al., 2014), and the classical error-analysis tradition argues that errors should be categorised by linguistic type rather than merely counted (Corder, 1967). The prior lip-reading study grouped its errors into contextual, phonetic, and homophone categories and found homophones about 1% of the mistakes (Hossain et al., under review), but a fuller staged analysis of the decoder's errors has not been reported. This project applies a three-stage framework, moving from surface metrics through phoneme-level confusion to a lexical, contextual, and semantic classification, to both a clean-trained and a noise-augmented decoder.

## 4.6 Legal, social, ethical, and professional issues

Working with lip-reading technology and with a corpus of recorded faces raises issues that go beyond accuracy.

The dataset carries legal and privacy obligations. LRS2 is drawn from BBC broadcast material and released for non-commercial academic research only, under a data-sharing agreement that must be signed before access (Chung et al., 2017). The recordings show identifiable people speaking, which is personal data, so their use is governed by data-protection law and the terms of that agreement. This project stays within those terms, does not redistribute the corpus, and works only with the phoneme and text transcriptions rather than the video, limiting the handling of identifiable imagery.

Socially and ethically, lip-reading is dual-use. The capability that helps a deaf or speech-impaired person communicate, or recovers speech in noise, can also read speech from surveillance video without the speaker's knowledge or consent. The assistive uses are a clear social good and the motivation for this work; the surveillance uses are a genuine risk, and the responsible position is to state plainly that the technology is dual-use and that deployment should require the subject's awareness and consent. Bias is a further consideration: LRS2 is British television speech, so a model trained on it reflects British English and the demographics of that material, and its accuracy on other accents and populations should not be assumed.

Professionally, the work follows the expectations of reproducible and honest research. Results are reported on a deduplicated evaluation set so the headline numbers are not inflated by memorised sentences, generation settings are recorded so runs can be reproduced, and a fixed random seed lets a run be repeated exactly. Reporting an honest lower figure in preference to a higher but contaminated one is itself a professional-conduct decision.

## 4.7 Synthesis and gaps

The reviewed work supports three positions. First, the two-stage phoneme-based design is an effective and analysable structure for lip-reading, and the phoneme-to-text stage is the part that most limits sentence-level accuracy. Second, language models are well suited to that stage, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning makes it practical to adapt a large model to it. Third, surface metrics alone give an incomplete and sometimes misleading picture of a decoder's quality.

Against this, four gaps remain, each mapping to a research question and a design decision. Decoder-only language models have not been evaluated for this task on this corpus, despite being the dominant modern design; this project fine-tunes Llama-3.2-3B and compares it against the published encoder-decoder result. High accuracy on LRS2 is reported on the standard split without accounting for sentence overlap between training and evaluation; this project audits that overlap and reports a deduplicated figure. The robustness of the stage to imperfect front-end input has not been studied, and augmentation, standard elsewhere in speech recognition, has not been applied to it; this project introduces noise-augmented training and measures the result. Finally, the assumption that homophones are a central error source has had only limited testing; this project applies a staged error analysis to two models and tests it directly. The chapters that follow develop the experiments that address these gaps.
