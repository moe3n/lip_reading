# Chapter 11. Comparison and Discussion

This chapter interprets the results and returns them to the four research questions. The held-out test set provides the headline figures for the clean-trained and noise-augmented models; the deduplicated validation set is used for the error analysis and the finer model-to-model comparisons run during development.

## 11.1 Model performance and the answer to RQ1

The first research question asked whether fine-tuning a decoder-only large language model outperforms prompting the same model, and how it compares with published encoder-decoder results. The answer to the first part is unambiguous. Prompted without training, the Llama-3.2-3B model reaches about 0.2% exact match and a word error rate above 100%, generating fluent but unrelated text. The no-language-model baseline, a small recurrent network trained from scratch, reaches essentially zero exact match, confirming that the task is not learnable from this data without prior language knowledge. Fine-tuning the same Llama model with QLoRA raises exact match to 92.0% on the deduplicated validation set. Fine-tuning is the lever that makes the task solvable, which model scale or prompting alone does not, and this is the clearest result of the project.

The comparison with published encoder-decoder work is decisive on the held-out test set. The prior study fine-tuned a Flan-T5 Large model to 85.11% exact match and a 3.23% word error rate on the standard test set (Hossain et al., under review). On the same standard test set, the decoder-only model here reaches 88.82% exact match and a 2.47% word error rate, exceeding the encoder-decoder result on both metrics by a clear margin. After deduplication its exact match is 87.44%, which still exceeds the published figure, even though that figure was itself computed on the contaminated standard split. This answers the question that motivated the choice of architecture: a current decoder-only model, fine-tuned with QLoRA, outperforms the encoder-decoder model on this task, at a smaller adapter parameter count and on a single graphics card.

## 11.2 Deduplicated evaluation and the answer to RQ2

The second research question asked how much of the accuracy is genuine once overlap between training and evaluation is removed. The exploratory analysis found that roughly one evaluation sentence in eight appears verbatim in training, and the model scores a perfect result on those by memorisation. On the held-out test set, the standard exact match of 88.82% falls to 87.44% once the 152 memorised sentences are removed, a gap of 1.4 points, and the validation set shows the same pattern. The gap is not large, but it is real, and its importance is its principle more than its size: a headline number on the standard split overstates the model's ability to generalise, and the overstatement is invisible unless the overlap is measured.

This point extends beyond the present work. The contamination is a property of the corpus and its standard split, so it affects any result on that split, including the 85.11% encoder-decoder figure, computed on the same standard test set that this project found to contain 152 memorised sentences. The deduplicated comparison is therefore the sounder one for both models, and reporting it is the project's answer to the second research question and one of its methodological contributions.

## 11.3 Robustness and the answer to RQ3

The third research question asked how robust the decoder is to the imperfect phonemes a visual front-end produces, and whether noise-augmented training improves that robustness. The clean-trained decoder is fragile: at a 5% corruption rate its exact match falls from around 93% on clean input to between 19% and 46%, depending on the type of corruption. This matters because a decoder in a complete system never receives clean phonemes; it receives the output of a front-end that makes exactly these errors. A decoder evaluated only on clean input is therefore measured under conditions it will not meet, and its clean-input accuracy overstates its usefulness in a pipeline.

Noise-augmented training addresses this: the decoder trained with corrupted phonemes holds between 58% and 81% exact match under the same 5% corruption, a large improvement. The cost is a few points of exact match on clean input: on the held-out test set exact match falls from 88.82% to 86.08% (87.44% to 84.14% deduplicated), the same roughly three-point gap seen on the deduplicated validation set, where it falls from 92.0% to 88.7%. For any deployment where the input will be imperfect, which is every real lip-reading system, this trade favours the noise-augmented decoder. The answer to the third research question is that the clean-trained decoder is not robust, that noise-augmented training substantially improves robustness, and that the clean-input cost is small enough to be worth paying when the decoder is used in a pipeline rather than on clean transcriptions.

The error analysis clarified what the cost actually is. The two models fail on the same hard cases, the rare words and proper nouns that neither can handle, and the extra failures noise training introduces are almost all wrong real words rather than garbled output. Per failure, the two models preserve meaning about equally, so the cost of robustness is a modest increase in confident wrong-word errors on clean input, well short of a collapse in quality.

## 11.4 The role of homophones and the answer to RQ4

The fourth research question asked whether homophones are the dominant error type. They are not. Across both models, homophones are around 5% of the failures by a strict dictionary definition and 8% to 10% by a broader one including near-identical sound-alikes. The dominant categories are wrong real words and a mixed group of non-words, rare names, and formatting issues, none of which a homophone-specific method would address. This corroborates the earlier study on this task, which found homophone confusions about 1% of the errors of a Flan-T5 decoder (Hossain et al., under review), and reaching the same conclusion with a different architecture strengthens it. A parallel prompting-only study on the same corpus reached a compatible conclusion, reporting homophone errors as a small share of the mistakes of a prompted Llama-3.1 model. The consistent finding across a prompted model, an encoder-decoder fine-tune, and a decoder-only fine-tune is that homophones are a minor error source, and effort is better spent on the wrong-word and out-of-vocabulary errors that dominate.

## 11.5 Comparison with prompting-only work

A peer study ran in parallel, using the same corpus but prompting a larger Llama-3.1 model without any fine-tuning. It reached a word error rate above 100%, in the same range as the zero-shot baseline here, and applied a similar error analysis. Together the two projects form a controlled comparison of prompting against fine-tuning on the same task: prompting a larger model does not solve it, while fine-tuning a smaller model does. This supports the central claim that the task requires adaptation, which scale and prompting alone do not provide, and it is stronger for being reached by two independent studies. The two also agree that homophones are a minor source of error, which adds weight to that conclusion.

## 11.6 Threats to validity

Several threats to validity were identified, and each is either mitigated or stated as a limitation. The most serious, contamination between training and evaluation, is mitigated by the deduplication procedure and by reporting the deduplicated figure. A second is speaker overlap: the transcriptions carry no speaker identifiers, so it cannot be ruled out that the same speakers appear in training and evaluation, which would make the task easier than a speaker-independent setting; this cannot be measured from the available data and is stated as a limitation. A third, that a difference between two runs might reflect chance or an uncontrolled setting rather than a real effect, was addressed by fixing the random seed and recording the decoding strategy, the control that allowed the decoding difference to be diagnosed rather than misattributed. A fourth is the small error sample, 76 and 107 failing sentences, so several finer breakdowns rest on single-digit counts; these are flagged where they occur, and the conclusions drawn from them are limited to what the counts support.

## 11.7 Limitations and future directions

The work has clear limitations. The error analysis and the finer model-to-model breakdowns are on the validation set and have not been extended to the held-out test set that provides the headline figures. The work also uses a single model size on a single graphics card, so the effect of a larger decoder or a different adapter rank is untested. These limitations, and the future directions they point to, including integrating the decoder with a real visual front-end so that the robustness result can be measured on genuine front-end errors rather than simulated ones, are developed in the conclusion.
